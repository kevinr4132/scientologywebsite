Issues

The hard questions, and how the Church answers them

Scientology has faced controversy since its earliest days. Here are the questions people ask most, along with the Church's response.

Why does the Church oppose psychiatry?

When Dianetics was introduced as a "mental therapy," physicians and psychiatrists accused the Church of practicing medicine without a license. Church leaders answered that psychiatry denies the spiritual side of human nature. This began a long conflict with the American Psychiatric Association over consciousness-altering drugs and procedures like lobotomies, carried on today by the Church's Citizens Commission on Human Rights. (Melton)

Is it really a religion?

In 1958 the IRS began revoking the tax-exempt status of Scientology churches, partly because they sold counseling courses, and in 1963 the FDA raided the Church in Washington, D.C., and seized its E-meters. (Melton) Some scholars argue Hubbard emphasized religion for practical reasons; scholar James R. Lewis concludes that, whatever Hubbard intended, Scientology became a religion nevertheless. (Lewis) In 1993 the Church won its fight with the IRS, ending the longest investigation in IRS history, which the Church saw as vindication. (Melton)

What was the Guardian's Office?

In 1966 the Church created the Guardian's Office to defend itself. Some of its leaders approved a plan to infiltrate U.S. government agencies, and its agents were arrested in 1979 and convicted. According to the Church, they had violated Church policy, and after an investigation, those involved were removed and the office was disbanded. (Melton)

Why do some countries reject it?

Germany and France have refused to recognize Scientology as a religion. German officials asked their domestic intelligence agency to investigate the Church in 1997 and 2007, and in 2009 a French court convicted Church officials of fraud, though it did not order the Church to stop its activities. (Melton)

What about former members and leaked materials?

Some former members have become strong critics, accusing the Church of financial fraud, harassing journalists, and acting vindictively against former members. (Melton) Confidential OT materials have been posted online, which the Church considers stolen sacred scripture. Beginning with a 1993 lawsuit, the Church has fought a series of legal battles over these postings, and in 2008 the Internet group Anonymous launched attacks against it. (Urban) (Lewis)

How many members are there, really?

The Church reports millions; independent surveys report far fewer. See Demographics for both numbers.
